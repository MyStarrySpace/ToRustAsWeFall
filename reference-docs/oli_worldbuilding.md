# Oli: Worldbuilding, Plot, and Research

Working document for Oli's character arc, the maintenance system's history, and the real-world research that grounds it. Main GDD has their biology and personality. This document tracks the institutional world they live in and the research informing it.

Method notes shared with the Tyreg document apply here too: no character lectures, Simon's rule from The Wire, Disco Elysium's internal voices as the model for how Oli's skills would speak when they's the active character. See tyreg_worldbuilding.md, Method Notes section.

## Quick Reference

Oli is an oligodendrocyte. Their role is to insulate and support the signal lines of the civilization: the electrical and metabolic infrastructure that carries communication between parts. In game terms they are the Defensive Support who thinks in networks and metabolism. They joined the maintenance class early, was given the tools to maintain what others built, and eventually made triage decisions during infrastructure failures that cut connections they knew mattered. They carry guilt for those choices. The arc is learning that the triage was the symptom of a system that defunded prevention, not a personal moral failure. Full character interiority is in the GDD character section.

## The Maintenance System as Institution

### The craft was once about prevention and then it became about response

The maintenance system Oli was trained into existed, generations ago, as a genuinely preventative craft. Workers walked routes looking for conditions that might become failures and fixed them before they failed. Insulation was replaced on a schedule based on material fatigue, not on what had already burned out. Atmospheric filters were checked and serviced before contamination levels reached critical. Conduits were rewrapped when the wrap showed wear, not when the signal degraded. The craft included a body of knowledge about how to read conditions that had not yet become problems, which is a form of knowledge that cannot be taught in a classroom. You acquire it by spending years inside a system that is still healthy enough to have conditions worth reading.

Oli did not work in that system. They worked in its successor. The craft knowledge was still present in the training materials and in the older workers who taught them. The conditions for applying that knowledge were gone. There were not enough workers to walk the routes. There was not enough material to replace insulation on schedule. There was not enough time to service atmospheric filters before they failed. Every shift was spent responding to failures that had already happened. The workers learned to prioritize which failures to respond to first, because there were always more failures than workers. The skill that defined the job shifted from "read conditions before they become failures" to "decide which failures to accept."

The older workers who trained Oli knew the difference. They taught the craft of prevention as an ideal while the workers practiced the craft of triage every day. Oli learned both. They understood that their triage was not what the job was supposed to be. They did not understand that the gap between what the job was supposed to be and what the job had become was not their fault, or even their generation's fault. It was a decision made upstream of them, by people who never set foot in the maintenance corridors, that preventative funding was an expense and reactive funding was a necessity. The reactive funding was always available because there were always failures. The preventative funding was always cuttable because you could not prove in advance which preventions had worked.

This is the structural core of the maintenance system's erosion: prevention produces absence of failure, which is invisible, which is impossible to budget for, which gets cut first when budgets tighten. Reactive work produces visible failures that need visible responses, which is easy to budget for, which grows to fill whatever is left after prevention is cut. Over generations, every maintenance system that does not actively protect its preventative funding ends up doing only response. The transition is not marked by any single decision. It is the accumulation of years of reasonable adjustments, each one defensible on its own, that together reduce the system to a fire brigade that cannot go home because there is always another fire.

### Triage becomes the job and the workers carry the guilt

When all you can do is respond, every response is a choice about which failures to accept. Oli did not become a triage worker because they liked making those choices. They became one because the conditions made every other kind of maintenance impossible. The workers who made these choices carried the weight of them in a specific way: they could name the people whose signals they cut. The person who decided not to fund the prevention never had to name anyone, because the failures the prevention would have stopped never happened, and absence of a failure does not have a name.

The asymmetry of names is the specific cruelty. A worker who triages has to look at the people affected by the triage. The executive who cut the preventative budget does not. The worker's guilt is concrete because the worker's decisions are concrete. The executive's decisions are abstract and statistical and do not produce named victims, because the victims are distributed across the population of people who would have been fine if the prevention had been funded. You cannot feel guilty about a counterfactual. The worker at the failure point absorbs the entire moral weight of the failure, even though the failure was architected by decisions made years earlier by people who never met the affected parties.

Oli knows this intellectually in the present-day game. They have had years to think about it. What they do not know is how to feel it. Their guilt is still organized around the specific faces of the people whose signals they cut. The abstract architecture of defunding does not have a face, and they cannot redirect grief at an abstraction. So they carry the grief for the named victims and the people who defunded the prevention carry nothing, because they have already retired or moved to other positions or simply never thought about it again. The grief concentrates at the bottom of the hierarchy, where the worker stands, because grief obeys gravity.

### Each generation of maintenance workers is more demoralized than the last

Oli is not the first generation of maintenance workers to experience this. They are several generations into the erosion. The older workers who trained them carried their own versions of the same guilt. They saw their teachers burn out, quit, drink, or adapt by numbing themselves to the failures they could not prevent. Oli's generation is the one where the adaptation has become so normalized that the workers no longer notice they are adapting. They walk into a corridor, read the signs of failure, decide which failure to address first, and do not register that this is triage rather than maintenance. The triage IS the maintenance, to them. The older concept of preventative maintenance is a story told by their teachers about conditions they themselves never experienced.

This is a specific kind of institutional forgetting that the GDD already touches on through the "Culture Growing" book fragment beat (where the civilization has lost the human concept of culture and retained only the biological one). Oli's generation has lost the concept of preventative maintenance as a practiced craft and retained only the word. They know what the word means. They can define it. They have never done it. When one of them attempts to practice it on their own time (Myke's hidden schematic, the elegant cable routing in the Processing Stacks that nobody assigned) it is classified as above-classification work and flagged for review. The craft persists in the margins, hidden, because the institution cannot accommodate it without admitting that the normal work is not maintenance at all.

### The blame flows downward because gravity is a design choice

When something fails, the institution needs a person to blame. The person blamed is always the one closest to the failure at the moment it occurred. This is not an accident. It is how bureaucratic liability is designed: the further you are from the point of failure, the less liability attaches to you. The executive who cut the preventative budget five years ago has no liability because the causal chain is too long to establish in any procedural way. The worker who made the triage decision last Tuesday has complete liability because the causal chain is short and visible. Blame follows proximity, and the institution arranges itself so that the decision-makers are never proximate to the failures their decisions produce.

Oli has been blamed. The people whose signals they cut, or their families, found them. Some of them understood that they were doing what they could with what they had. Most of them needed someone to be angry at, and they were the person standing in front of the failure, so they were angry at them. They absorbed it. They did not defend themself because defending themself would have required explaining the structural cause of the triage, and explaining the structural cause would have sounded like blaming someone else for their own choices, and they could not bring themself to do that in front of the people they had failed. So they took the blame. Over years. From many people. The blame accumulated in them the way the triage decisions did, and by the time the game starts they have built their identity around being the person who makes hard choices and absorbs the consequences.

What they have never done is ask whether the choices had to be hard. They have never asked whether the conditions that forced the triage were themselves choices that someone else made. The book fragment they will eventually encounter (Sen's Development as Freedom) contains the vocabulary for asking that question, but Oli does not immediately recognize themself in it. They read Sen's Annapurna parable and understands it as a discussion of triage ethics. They do not yet see that Sen is not asking which person Annapurna should help. Sen is asking why Annapurna has to choose at all.

## Oli at the Bottom of the Maintenance Hierarchy

### Their specific position in the system

The maintenance system has a class structure that mirrors the civilization's broader stratification. Senior engineers design systems they will never touch. Supervisors schedule work they will never do. Workers maintain infrastructure built by people who are no longer employed and designed by people who were never physically present in the spaces the infrastructure occupies. Oli is at the bottom of this hierarchy, in the category of worker who actually touches the material. This is the class Sennett would recognize as the craftsman, and it is also the class the institution has spent generations classifying as low-status manual labor.

The classification is the key move. The institution needs the work done but does not want to acknowledge the work as skilled, because acknowledging it as skilled would require paying for it as skilled, and paying for it as skilled would consume the budget currently allocated to the executive layer. So the institution teaches the workers that their work is manual labor requiring only physical capability, even though the work actually requires decades of accumulated embodied judgment about how specific materials fail in specific conditions. The workers absorb the classification because they hear it from every source of authority in their lives. Oli absorbed it. They do not think of themself as a craftsman. They think of themself as a maintenance worker, which is what the institution told them they are.

Their dry humor and refusal to dramatize anything are the social behaviors of someone who has decided that asking to be taken seriously is not worth the energy. They file observations like incident reports because incident reports are the one format where their observations are treated as valid. Everything else they notice, they keep to themself, because the institution has no category for a maintenance worker having insights. When Aster starts treating their observations as insights rather than just data points, Oli does not quite know how to respond. Nobody has done that to them in a long time.

### Why Oli is the one who triages rather than refuses

Other workers in Oli's situation have quit, burned out, numbed themselves, or adapted in other ways. Oli stayed and kept making the choices. The reason is specific to their character: they believe that if they stop making the choices, no choices will be made, and the absence of choice will produce more damage than the presence of bad choice. They are right about this in the immediate term. In the absence of a triage worker, the failures would compound. They are wrong about it in the long term, because the presence of a functioning triage worker allows the institution to continue defunding prevention without consequence. Oli is the person whose competence enables the system to keep getting worse without collapsing, which means their competence is part of the system's self-sustaining failure.

They do not know this when the game starts. If someone told them this directly, they would reject it, because it would sound like an accusation that their care was causing harm. The only way they can come to see it is by encountering a specific situation where their preventative action makes a specific difference, and then tracing backward to realize that every triage decision they made was a situation where someone could have done that preventative action earlier and nobody did. The arc is not about them stopping the triage. It is about them recognizing that the triage is a symptom, which frees them from the guilt of believing it was a personal moral failing.

### The incident that made them a fugitive

Before the game begins, Oli was working maintenance on a section of the facility that housed simulation equipment. Their specific training is in electrical systems; they are an electrician by trade. This matters for the incident because it defines the shape of what they could and could not do. Electricians are trained to read failure points across systems that border theirs. An electrician walking through a building will notice structural issues, HVAC problems, plumbing failures, and roof damage, because all of those things eventually become electrical problems when water reaches wiring or when a ceiling collapses onto a panel. But noticing is not the same as having authority to fix. The structural issue above the simulation equipment was not in Oli's scope. They could see it. They could file reports about it. They could not repair it themself.

They had been filing repair requests on the structural issue for an extended period. The requests were logged but not actioned. This is the normal shape of the system: requests route to the department with authority to fix the thing, and that department has its own backlog, its own resource constraints, its own prioritization decisions. Oli's requests sat in someone else's queue. Oli had also been increasingly vocal in worker channels about the underlying pattern: the simulation builders were directing resources toward new features and promotional events designed to attract more users into the simulation, while preventative maintenance budgets were being cut to fund that growth. They were saying what the other workers were thinking, which is a different kind of exposure than thinking it quietly.

The pattern they were describing was real and visible throughout the facility. New dining halls and promotional-facing construction were absorbing capital budgets while existing buildings were allowed to degrade. Users and workers in the facility had been complaining about mold, repeated flooding, broken climate control, and general infrastructure failure across multiple structures. The complaints were received and filed but did not translate into reallocation. The institution's growth strategy required the appearance of investment to attract new users, and investment in existing-infrastructure maintenance does not photograph well for recruitment materials. The visible new construction had to continue. The invisible maintenance had to be cut. Oli was describing this pattern out loud because they were an electrician who had been walking through every degrading section of the facility for years and had the specific professional vantage point to see the whole pattern clearly.

The ceiling collapsed. The damage to the simulation equipment was severe, and the financial loss was significant enough that someone had to be responsible in a way the institution could publicly assign. The repair requests Oli had filed were in the system. Management had a choice about whether to surface those requests as evidence of systemic neglect, or to bury them and frame the incident as the act of an individual worker. The institution did what institutions do. Oli's complaints about resource allocation, which existed in worker channels and in some written communications, were recharacterized as motive. The collapse, which was caused by deferred maintenance they had explicitly warned about, was reframed as sabotage they had caused.

The framing worked because the alternative reading would have required the institution to acknowledge that its own funding decisions had produced the collapse. Admitting that would have cost leadership more than blaming Oli cost them. The maintenance worker at the bottom of the hierarchy is always available as a scapegoat, because the institution has already classified their labor as low-status manual work, which makes it easy to classify them as someone who might act out of resentment. The resentment was real, because the conditions were real. The sabotage was not. But the institution only needed the first to make the second believable.

There is a specific cruelty to what happened with their paper trail. Oli had done their job correctly. They had filed the reports, flagged the hazards, documented that they saw them. The paper trail of their diligence became the paper trail of their motive. The same records that proved they had been warning about the failure for months could be reinterpreted, by people with the authority to interpret them, as proof that they had been building a case about institutional neglect specifically so that their later sabotage could be justified. The more reports they had filed, the more evidence of premeditation. The workers who do their jobs thoroughly generate the cleanest paper trails, which makes them the most frame-able when the institution needs someone to blame. Workers who did nothing are harder to frame because there is nothing to reinterpret.

Oli ran. They did not fight the charge through official channels because official channels were the mechanism producing the charge. Fighting it would have meant trusting that a system that had just defunded prevention and then blamed them for the predictable consequences would now fairly adjudicate their culpability for those consequences. They did not trust this, correctly. The institution would have closed ranks. Other maintenance workers who might have corroborated their warnings would have been pressured to stay quiet or risk being implicated themselves. The evidence of their repair requests existed in the system's logs, but logs can be interpreted in more than one way by people with the authority to interpret them.

They have been moving since then. The party encounters them as someone carrying both their triage guilt and a separate, newer weight: they were the worker who was right about what was going to happen, and their being right is what made them useful to blame. This compounds the earlier guilt rather than resolving it. They know intellectually that they did not sabotage anything. They also knows that their complaints about resource allocation were real, and that the institution used those complaints as the motive for an action they did not take. The framing was false but it was not arbitrary. They could only frame them because they had been saying the thing they needed them to be punished for saying.

The fugitive status shapes how they move through the world in the present-day game. They avoid institutional checkpoints when possible. They do not use terminals that might log their presence unless they are reasonably sure the logging infrastructure in that section has degraded enough to be unreliable. They read infrastructure for failure points not only from their craft habit but from a new habit of needing to know where institutional surveillance is still functional and where it is not. Their incident-report voice and their dry humor now have an additional layer of function: they are the verbal posture of someone who has learned that how you describe what happened is sometimes more important than what happened. They describe things flatly because any other register could be turned into evidence of something.

The arc implication: when Oli eventually recognizes that their triage work was a symptom of systemic defunding rather than a personal moral failing, the recognition also reaches the sabotage charge. The institution framed them for the collapse their warnings predicted, and the reason they could frame them was the same reason their triage work felt like personal failure: the system routes blame downward because the alternative is acknowledging that the architecture above is designed to produce exactly these outcomes. Clearing their name, if it ever happens, requires exposing the architecture. That is not a personal vindication. It is a structural one, which is the kind Oli has always been skeptical they would ever receive, because structural vindication requires the institution to admit something it has every incentive not to admit.

## What Oli Doesn't Know

### They do not know their work was never supposed to be this hard

Oli has never experienced a maintenance system that was funded for prevention. They have no reference point for what the job is supposed to feel like. The conditions they works in are the only conditions they have known, which means they have calibrated their sense of "hard" against a baseline that is already catastrophically degraded. When they think "this is a difficult job," they means something specific that does not quite map onto what a maintenance worker three generations ago would have meant by the same phrase. Three generations ago, a difficult day was one where you found a condition you could not immediately diagnose. Today, a difficult day is one where you cannot reach all of the failures before some of them compound. These are not the same kind of difficulty. Oli has no way to know they are not the same because they have never experienced the earlier kind.

The revelation, when it comes, is not "you have been working too hard." It is "you have been working a job that used to be three jobs, and the other two jobs were the ones that would have prevented the failures you have been responding to." That reframing is what the game is working toward for them. It does not absolve the triage choices. It locates them inside a structure that made them inevitable, which is a different kind of knowledge than guilt.

### They do not know the civilian word for what they do

Oli's language gap is different from Tyreg's. Tyreg is missing the colloquial register because the academy filtered it out of her. Oli is missing something more specific: the word for their job that would describe what the job actually is. The institutional term for their work is "maintenance" and the institutional description is "keeping the infrastructure running." Both of those are technically accurate and both of them obscure what they actually does, which is deciding who lives and who loses their signal. The institution does not have a word for that because giving it a word would make it visible, and making it visible would raise questions about why the job exists in that form.

When civilians encounter Oli, they call them a maintenance worker. When they are angry at them, they call them the person who cut their connection. Neither of those phrases names what they do at the full scale of it. They have spent their career without a word for their own job. When Sen's Annapurna parable gives them the phrase "tragic choice," it hits them specifically because it is the first time anyone has named the thing they do in language that acknowledges the moral weight of it. They do not say anything when they read the passage. They close the book and sits for a while. The party does not know what is happening to them.

### They do not recognize themself in the book fragment

The book fragment scene where the party finds Sen's Development as Freedom is the first time Oli speaks voluntarily about their past. The GDD already covers this. What the worldbuilding adds is the specific nature of what they do not recognize in Sen's argument. Sen is not asking how Annapurna should choose. Sen is asking why Annapurna has to choose, which Sen frames as a question about development. Development in Sen's sense means the removal of unfreedoms, the expansion of capabilities, the creation of conditions where tragic choice is no longer necessary. Oli reads this and understands it as a philosophical position. They do not yet understand that it describes their own situation: the tragic choices they make are not philosophical problems but the consequences of a failure to develop in Sen's sense.

The recognition comes later, in a specific moment (see the scenes below). It does not come from Sen directly. It comes from encountering a specific preventative action that worked, and tracing the chain backward to realize what kind of world Sen was describing.

## Plot Threads and Scene Ideas

### The triage flashback

The setup is a present-day party scene where Oli has to place a barrier. The party is in a compromised corridor. Multiple threats are approaching from multiple directions. Oli can only place one barrier. The player selects where to place it. Whatever direction Oli blocks, the other directions are exposed. Peris, Aster, or another party member is going to take damage from the direction Oli did not cover. The player makes the choice.

The choice triggers a flashback. Oli, younger, walking into a corridor full of people in pods. The infrastructure is failing. They have one working insulation spool and an unknown number of failing conduits. They do not have time to rewrap all of them. They stand at the junction for a moment and decide. They rewrap the conduits carrying the signals that the system's records identify as highest priority. They walk past the other conduits knowing they will fail within days.

The camera stays on their hands while they works. The hands are steady, precise, practiced. The craft is visible in the motion. The tragedy is that the craft is being applied inside a constraint that makes the craft feel cruel. They finish the rewrap. They test the connections. They nod to themself the way they do in the present-day game when they finish a task. Then they walk past the conduits they left unwrapped, and the flashback ends on their back receding down the corridor.

There is no dialogue in the flashback. Oli does not explain their choice to anyone because there is nobody to explain it to. The other workers are elsewhere, making their own triage decisions in their own corridors. The people in the pods cannot see them. The institution is not watching. They make the choice alone and carries it alone, which is the specific cruelty of the situation: the triage is done in isolation so that when the consequences emerge, the worker is the only person the consequences can find.

Present-day Oli is standing over the barrier they just placed. The party is in position. The threats the barrier covers are being handled. The threats on the other side are coming. Oli does not say anything about the flashback. Peris might notice something changed in their posture, or might not. The scene moves on.

### The consequence they meet in person

Later in the game, the party encounters a person whose signal was cut. Not in a confrontational scene. In a mundane one. The person is a client of Peris, or someone living in a sheltered corner of the Residential Rings, or a civilian going about whatever life they have left. The person is diminished in a specific way: they respond slowly, they repeat themselves, they have lost some of the connections that used to make them who they were. They are still alive. They are still a person. They are less than they used to be, in ways that matter to them.

Peris recognizes the person and greets them. Oli sees the pattern of the diminishment and their data layer lights up with the specific signature of a lost conduit connection. They know what this is. They might even know exactly when it happened. The person does not recognize Oli because the person never saw the worker who made the decision; they only saw their own slow loss of function afterward.

The scene is entirely about what Oli does with this recognition. They do not confess. They do not apologize. Apologizing would be about making themself feel better, and they have trained themself out of that particular form of self-indulgence. They just see the person. They stay in the conversation that Peris is having with them. They do not say their own name unless asked. When the party leaves, Oli walks a few steps behind the others. They do not say what they are thinking. Peris might notice. Myke definitely notices and does not comment because Myke understands the shape of this kind of silence from his own life.

The scene has no resolution. Oli is going to carry this encounter. The player watches them carry it. Nobody in the party narrates the theme.

### The preventative action that works

The crossover moment for Oli is not a speech. It is a scene where they perform a preventative action, possibly for the first time in their career, and watch it work.

The setup could be a Zone 3 shelter area where Peris has been tending flora that is competing with a Candid colony. The flora is winning but slowly. Oli is resting at the shelter. They notice a specific pattern in the flora's growth that their data layer recognizes as the beginning of an overload condition: the flora's signal conduction is going to outpace its metabolic capacity, and when it does, the flora will die and the Candid will reclaim the area. They have seen this pattern before, late, as a failure they had to respond to. They are seeing it now, early, as a condition they could fix.

They get up. They walk to the flora. They do something small. A rewrap. A targeted insulation. A redirection of some conduit that nobody else in the party would have noticed. The fix takes them thirty seconds. They go back to their rest.

The flora does not die. The Candid does not reclaim the area. Peris notices, after a while, that the flora has stabilized and did not follow the normal pattern she was expecting. She asks Oli if they did something. They shrug. They say something like "I fixed a thing." They do not elaborate. They do not know how to explain what they did, not because the action was complicated, but because the action was so small and the consequence was so large that explaining it would sound like bragging, and they do not know how to brag.

The moment that matters for their arc is the one after Peris walks away. Oli sits back down and looks at the flora. They trace backward, the way craftsmen trace backward. If they had done that rewrap thirty seconds earlier in any of the corridors where they triaged, the failure would not have happened. The failures were not failures. They were absences of thirty-second rewraps. The reason they did not do the rewraps was that they did not have thirty seconds to spare, because the institution never gave them those thirty seconds, because giving them those thirty seconds would have required funding that went to someone else. The triage was not the work. The triage was the work the institution paid for, and the work the institution did not pay for was what would have prevented the triage from ever being necessary.

They do not say any of this out loud. They just sits and looks at the flora. The player watches them sit. The scene moves on.

### The callback: Oli does a preventative action for a person

Late in the game, there is a parallel scene where Oli encounters someone who is in the early stages of the same kind of signal degradation they used to triage. Not a stranger. Someone the player knows. Maybe a civilian NPC the party has helped before, maybe one of Peris's clients. The signs are there, in Oli's data overlay, of a conduit that is going to fail if nothing is done. The person does not know. The person is still themselves, still functional, still doing whatever they were doing when the party arrived.

Oli does the thirty-second fix. They do not tell the person what they are doing. They do not tell the party what they are doing. They make it look like incidental maintenance, like something any infrastructure worker would do as a matter of course, and then they walk away. The person continues their life. They will never know that Oli just prevented something that would have taken parts of their self away from them.

This is the callback to the triage flashback. In the flashback, Oli walked past the conduits they were not going to save, with their back to the people whose signals were about to fail. In the callback, Oli walks away from a fix nobody saw, with their back to a person who does not know anything happened. The two images are the same physical action, the worker walking away from an intervention, but the meanings are inverted. The first walk was Oli carrying guilt for a failure the institution had made inevitable. The second walk is Oli doing work the institution was never going to pay for, for reasons they have finally reframed to themself. They are not absolved. They are just no longer alone with the weight.

### The "that's how mine looked" beat for Oli

Oli's equivalent of the Tyreg/Aster recognition moment from her document is probably between Oli and Myke. Myke has the elegant hidden cable routing in the Processing Stacks. That work is a form of above-classification craftsmanship that Myke hid because the institution would have flagged it. Oli recognizes the work when the party revisits the Stacks with Myke in the party. They see how the routing was done. They understand that Myke did this on his own time, hiding from the institution, because Myke needed to do work that mattered and the assigned work did not.

Oli might say one line. Something like "nobody taught you to do that." Myke might say "no." They might not say anything else. The scene moves on. Two craftsmen have recognized each other across specializations, and the recognition is the moment Oli realizes their own hidden work (the thirty-second fixes they have started doing for people who will never know) is not unique. Other workers in other domains have been doing the same thing for the same reasons. The craft never died. It just went underground when the institution stopped funding it.

## Research Notes

This section is a scaffold for future research. The Tyreg document has a more developed version because the research has been done; Oli's research is ahead of us.

### Deferred maintenance and the economics of prevention

The concept of deferred maintenance in civil engineering and public infrastructure is the direct real-world analog for Oli's situation. When budgets tighten, preventative maintenance gets cut first because its absence is invisible. This produces a cascading effect where reactive repair costs grow to consume the available budget, which means even less is available for prevention, which means more reactive repair is needed. The ASCE Infrastructure Report Card is the canonical source for how this plays out in US bridges, roads, water systems, and similar. The pattern is consistent: funding is reactive, failures are predictable, and the workers at the point of failure absorb the blame for the structural underfunding.

Research targets: ASCE Report Card history, specific bridge collapse post-mortems (I-35W Mississippi River Bridge, Fern Hollow Bridge), the concept of "runtime" in infrastructure versus "design life," and the literature on how public infrastructure spending gets allocated between maintenance and new construction.

### Prevention versus response in public health

The public health parallel is sharper. Preventative public health produces the absence of disease, which is invisible and difficult to budget for, while reactive public health produces visible outcomes (hospitalizations, treatments, recoveries) that are easy to measure and justify. The consistent finding is that preventative spending is far more cost-effective than reactive spending, by orders of magnitude, and yet preventative spending is consistently underfunded. This is not a failure of knowledge. The knowledge has been extremely clear for decades. It is a failure of political incentive: politicians get credit for responding to visible crises and do not get credit for preventing crises that would have occurred if they had not prevented them.

Research targets: Tuberculosis control history, water fluoridation and dental health, lead paint remediation delay, the specific budget histories of the CDC and WHO, and the concept of "negative epidemiology" (measuring the effect of prevention by what did not happen).

### Moral injury in healthcare workers during triage events

The psychological literature on moral injury is the specific frame for Oli's guilt. Moral injury is distinct from PTSD: it is the specific harm caused by being forced to act against one's own moral standards because of situational constraints. Healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic were forced to triage ventilators, beds, and attention in ways that harmed patients, and many of those workers carry ongoing moral injury from choices that were not actually theirs in any meaningful sense. The literature is clear that moral injury is worsened by isolation: workers who processed their triage decisions alone carried more lasting damage than workers who processed them collectively, because collective processing provides the structural framing that individual processing cannot.

Research targets: the specific studies on COVID-era triage and moral injury, the work of Jonathan Shay on moral injury in combat veterans, the concept of "impossible ethics" in disaster medicine, and whether the research distinguishes between situations where the triage worker could have made a different choice and situations where they could not.

### Sen's capability approach and the framing of unfreedom

Sen's Development as Freedom is already in the GDD as a book fragment Oli encounters. The worldbuilding can go deeper into what Sen actually argues. Development in Sen's sense is not economic growth but the removal of specific unfreedoms that prevent people from living the lives they have reason to value. Triage, in Sen's framing, is an unfreedom: it constrains the worker's ability to act on their moral values, and it constrains the patients' ability to receive the care they would otherwise receive. The correct response to triage is not better triage but the elimination of the conditions that require it. This is the exact reframing Oli needs.

Research targets: the specific chapters in Development as Freedom that apply to Oli's situation (the Kerala chapter on public services, the discussion of entitlements and famines, the capability approach as distinct from utility and rights-based frameworks), and the Annapurna parable specifically. Also worth reading: Martha Nussbaum's development of the capability approach, which is sometimes more accessible than Sen's own writing.

### Ivan Illich on iatrogenesis and institutional harm

Illich's Medical Nemesis argues that modern medicine produces harm through its own institutional form, not just through individual error. The parallel for Oli is that maintenance institutions can produce harm through their own form: by classifying preventative work as optional and reactive work as essential, they create the conditions where the workers at the point of failure are the ones who absorb the moral weight of structural decisions made elsewhere. Illich is polemical and should be read carefully, but their framework for understanding institutional harm is useful for locating Oli's guilt inside a structural rather than personal account.

Research targets: Medical Nemesis specifically on iatrogenesis, Illich's concept of counterproductivity (institutions that produce the opposite of their stated goals once they reach a certain size), and whether Illich's framework has been extended to infrastructure maintenance specifically.

## Open Research Questions

The history of how specific preventative maintenance programs got defunded. Not the general pattern, but the specific case studies. Which state or national infrastructure programs had robust preventative budgets and then lost them, over what period, through what decisions. The Minnesota I-35W bridge is one example. There are likely others with clearer documentation.

The psychology of craftsmen who work in degraded institutional conditions. How do they maintain their skills when the institution does not value them? How do they teach younger workers? What does the transmission of craft knowledge look like when the institution actively obstructs it? Sennett is the obvious starting point but there may be more specific empirical work.

The history of the word "maintenance" and how its meaning has narrowed over time in English. Whether there was ever a period when "maintenance" meant "preventative care" as its primary referent, and when that primary referent shifted to "reactive repair." The semantic drift would parallel the worldbuilding narrative.

The literature on how organizations handle the blame flow after preventable failures. Who absorbs blame, who escapes it, what structural features predict which direction blame flows. This is adjacent to moral injury but is more specifically about bureaucratic dynamics than individual psychology.

The specific mechanics of how oligodendrocytes experience metabolic stress and how that maps to burnout. If Oli's iron cost mechanic is biologically accurate, then the psychological framework for their exhaustion should align with what is known about oligodendrocyte vulnerability in real neuroscience. Whether there are good analogies between cellular metabolic exhaustion and human caregiver burnout.

Comparative case studies of maintenance systems that did NOT erode into triage. Are there any? Where, when, and under what conditions. Finland and the Nordic model come up in the Tyreg research. The parallel question for Oli is whether any industrial society has maintained a functioning preventative maintenance infrastructure across generations, and if so what protected it.
